## Sizing & Fit

**Q: Do your jackets run small?**

A: Some of them do, and the one to watch is the Wind River Hardshell ($240). It runs about a half-size small, so if you plan to layer underneath it — a fleece, a down jacket, or both — we'd recommend sizing up. If you'll mostly wear it over a base layer in milder conditions, your usual size is usually fine, but most people who buy a hardshell are buying it precisely to go over other layers, so sizing up is the safer call for the majority of customers. The rest of our jacket line runs true to size in our experience: the Basecamp Down Jacket and the Ridgeline Fleece both fit as you'd expect from your normal size. The Wind River is the one we always flag at the counter, and now you've been flagged too. When in doubt, come try it on with the layers you actually intend to wear under it, or call us and we'll talk it through.

**Q: How do I find the right size if I'm shopping online and can't try things on?**

A: Every product page on trailheadsupply.co has a detailed size chart with measurements in both inches and centimeters, and we'd strongly suggest measuring yourself rather than guessing from the tag in a piece of clothing you already own — brands vary enough that your "medium" in one label can be a "large" in another. For footwear, measure your foot late in the day, since feet swell a little as you walk and stand, and measure both feet, because most people have one slightly larger than the other; size to the bigger one. Wear or hold the socks you actually plan to hike in when you measure, because a thick wool sock changes the math. For packs, sizing is about torso length, not your shirt size, so use the pack's specific fit guide. If you're still between sizes or want a second opinion, email hello@trailheadsupply.co with the exact item and your measurements and we'll point you the right way before you order.

**Q: I'm between two sizes — should I go up or down?**

A: It depends on the item and how you'll use it, so here's our rule of thumb. For boots and trail shoes, you want snug-but-not-tight with room to wiggle your toes and no heel slip; going up a half size is often the safer call once you account for thicker hiking socks and a little foot swelling on long days. For layering pieces, size up if you'll wear things underneath — this is doubly true for the Wind River Hardshell, which already runs a half-size small. For the Ridgeline Fleece worn on its own, your true size is usually most comfortable. For packs, "between sizes" usually means your torso length falls on the boundary between two frames, in which case the fit guide and a quick call to us will settle it faster than guessing. When you genuinely can't decide, the shop floor beats the spreadsheet every time — come in and try both, since trying gear on in person is one of the best reasons to visit us at 214 Pine Street.

## Boots

**Q: What boots do you carry, and which one is right for me?**

A: We keep our boot lineup deliberately simple — three well-chosen options instead of an overwhelming wall of choices — and each one has a clear job. The **Granite Peak Hiker ($185)** is our all-rounder, built for day hikes and weekend backpacking on established trails; it's supportive, durable, and the boot most people walk out with because it covers the widest range of what folks actually do. The **Summit Pro Mountaineering Boot ($320)** is the serious one: a stiff, crampon-compatible boot made for alpine routes, glacier travel, snow, and genuine 4-season mountaineering, where you need rigidity and protection more than all-day softness. And the **Creekside Trail Shoe ($120)** is our lightweight low-cut option for fast, light days on easier terrain, trail running, and travel, where you'd rather have breathability and a nimble feel than heavy-duty support. If you tell us where you're headed and how far, we can almost always match you to the right one of these three — and if none of them fits your trip, we'll tell you that honestly too.

**Q: Are your boots waterproof, and how do I keep them dry?**

A: The Granite Peak Hiker and the Summit Pro both have waterproof, breathable membranes and are built to keep your feet dry through stream crossings, slush, wet brush, and rain. The Creekside Trail Shoe is intentionally more breathable and quick-drying than fully sealed, because the light-and-fast hikers it's made for usually prefer ventilation and fast drying over a waterproof membrane that traps heat. Keep in mind that no boot stays dry forever if water comes in over the top of the cuff, so for serious wet or snow you'll want to pair your footwear with the right socks and a set of gaiters. To keep the waterproofing working over time, clean off mud and reapply a leather or fabric waterproofing treatment when you notice water no longer beading on the surface — and never dry wet boots on a heater or by a fire, which cracks leather and degrades membranes.

**Q: How should I break in a new pair of boots before a big trip?**

A: Start slow and start early. Wear your new boots around the house and on short errands and walks for the first week or so, using the socks you'll actually hike in, so your feet and the boots can get acquainted before you ask them to carry you twelve miles. Then build up: a few longer walks, then a loaded day hike, before you commit them to a multi-day trip. The amount of break-in needed varies by boot — the Creekside Trail Shoe needs almost none and is close to ready out of the box, the Granite Peak Hiker needs a little, and the Summit Pro, being a stiff mountaineering boot, asks for the most patience. If you feel a hot spot starting on a walk, stop and address it immediately with a blister bandage rather than pushing through; a hot spot caught early is a non-event, and one ignored becomes a trip-ending blister.

**Q: Can I get my boots resoled when the tread wears out?**

A: Yes, and honestly this is one of our favorite things to do. Our in-house repair shop offers boot resoling starting at $55, and a good resole can give a quality pair of boots a whole second life rather than sending them to the landfill with plenty of upper left in them. Resole-friendly construction is one of the reasons we choose the boots we stock — we don't want to sell you something that's disposable by design. Bring your pair in or call to ask whether they're a good candidate; some heavily worn or lower-construction boots aren't worth resoling, and we'll give you a straight answer rather than taking on a job that won't serve you. See the Repairs section below for the full list of what we fix and what it costs.

## Jackets & Layers

**Q: What's the difference between your jackets, and which do I actually need?**

A: Think of our three layering pieces as three different jobs, and most active people in the mountains end up wanting all three for different conditions. The **Wind River Hardshell ($240)** is your weather shield: waterproof and windproof, it goes over everything to block rain and wind, but it has no insulation of its own — it's there to keep the weather out, not to keep you warm by itself. The **Basecamp Down Jacket ($199)** is your warmth layer: lightweight, highly packable down for cold, dry conditions, and it tucks neatly under the hardshell when things turn truly nasty. The **Ridgeline Fleece ($89)** is your everyday mid-layer: breathable, forgiving warmth for active days, and a comfortable standalone piece around camp or around town. If you're buying just one to start, buy the layer that matches your most common conditions — a hardshell if you mostly battle wind and rain, the fleece if you mostly want comfortable everyday warmth, the down if cold is your main problem.

**Q: Is the Basecamp Down Jacket warm enough for real winter, and how do I care for down?**

A: For cold, dry winter conditions, yes — the Basecamp Down Jacket is genuinely warm for its weight and packs down small enough to stash in a daypack, which is exactly what makes down so beloved. The one thing to understand about down is that it loses loft, and therefore warmth, when it gets wet, so in damp or snowy weather you'll want to keep it dry by wearing the Wind River Hardshell over the top. For high-output aerobic days, many people prefer to start a little cold and pull the down on at rest stops rather than sweating into it while moving. Caring for it is simpler than people fear: use a technical down-specific wash rather than regular detergent (which strips the natural oils that keep down lofting), run a gentle cycle in a front-loading machine, and — this is the critical part — tumble dry on low with a couple of clean dryer balls or tennis balls to break up the clumps and restore loft. It takes several cycles and some patience. If that sounds fiddly, bring it by the shop and we're happy to walk you through it.

**Q: Can I layer all three pieces together, and in what order?**

A: That's exactly how the system is designed to work, and the order matters. Closest to you goes the Ridgeline Fleece for breathable warmth; over that goes the Basecamp Down Jacket for insulation; and on the very top goes the Wind River Hardshell to block wind and rain and seal everything in. Because the Wind River runs about a half-size small, size it up if you intend to stack layers underneath — otherwise it'll pull tight over the down and squash the loft you're paying for, which actually makes you colder. Most three-season days you'll only need one or two of the layers, and the full three-piece stack is reserved for genuinely cold, exposed, windy conditions. Learning to add and shed layers before you're soaked in sweat or shivering is one of the real skills of comfortable mountain travel, and it's worth practicing on easy days.

## Packs & Camping Gear

**Q: What size pack should I get, and how should it fit?**

A: It comes down to how long you're out and how much gear you carry, and we offer three sizes to cover the spread. The **Daybreak 22L ($110)** is a daypack — perfect for day hikes, summit pushes, and carrying water, snacks, a layer, and the ten essentials. The **Trailhead 45L ($210)** is our overnight-to-weekend workhorse, big enough for a one-to-three-night trip if you pack thoughtfully, and it's the most versatile pack for the most people, so when in doubt this is the one. The **Expedition 65L ($280)** is for longer trips, bulkier winter gear, or hauling for two. As for fit, the single most important thing to understand is that pack sizing is about torso length, not your shirt size or height — a tall person can have a short torso and vice versa. Measure from the bony bump at the base of your neck to the line across your lower back at the top of your hip bones, then match that to the pack's fit guide. A well-fitted pack carries most of its weight on your hips, not your shoulders.

**Q: What tent do you carry, and who is it right for?**

A: Our three-season tent is the **Cirque 2 Tent ($350)**, a two-person freestanding backpacking tent that balances weight, livability, and weather protection — the three things that are always in tension in tent design. It's roomy enough for two people plus their gear, or downright luxurious for one person who likes elbow room, and it's light enough to carry into the backcountry without dreading the haul. Being freestanding, it's easy to pitch on rocky or hard ground where stakes won't bite, and you can pick it up and reposition it once it's assembled. For genuine winter and alpine trips that demand a sturdier, snow-shedding shelter, we don't sell a 4-season tent — instead we offer 4-season tents through our seasonal rental program, which lets you get the right shelter for a specific trip without buying a specialized tent you'd use twice a year. See the Rentals section for details.

**Q: How do I choose a sleeping bag and pad, and why does the pad matter so much?**

A: Match the bag's temperature rating to the coldest nights you realistically expect, then give yourself a little margin, because a temperature rating is closer to a survival number than a comfort number — most people sleep comfortably several degrees warmer than the rating suggests. The **Aspen 20° Sleeping Bag ($160)** is an excellent three-season choice, comfortable for the majority of spring-through-fall nights in the mountains around here. Pair it with the **Backcountry Sleeping Pad ($75)**, which does two jobs at once: it cushions you from the ground, and — just as importantly — it insulates you from it. This second job surprises people. A huge amount of body heat is lost downward into cold ground through a compressed sleeping bag, so without an insulating pad even a warm bag will leave you cold. Together the Aspen bag and Backcountry pad make a solid, affordable sleep system that handles the conditions most of our customers actually camp in.

**Q: Do you sell tent stakes, repair kits, and small spare parts?**

A: We do, and we keep them stocked precisely because of our repair-don't-replace philosophy — a $3 part can save a $350 tent, and a lost buckle shouldn't retire a perfectly good pack. ## Product Care & Warranty

**Q: How should I care for my gear so it actually lasts?**

A: A little routine maintenance goes a remarkably long way, and most of it is simple. Brush dirt and grit off boots after trips and let them dry slowly at room temperature, away from any direct heat source — never on a heater or by a fire, which cracks leather and weakens adhesives. Reproof waterproof jackets with a wash-in or spray-on treatment as soon as water stops beading on the surface, rather than waiting until they soak through. Store down and synthetic sleeping bags loosely in a large breathable storage sack rather than crammed into their compression stuff sack, so the insulation keeps its loft over the years. Always dry your tent completely before storing it, because a tent packed away even slightly damp will grow mildew that smells terrible and degrades the fabric. Do these few small things and most quality gear will outlast your expectations by years — which is exactly the point.

**Q: What's your warranty, and what if my gear is just worn out rather than defective?**

A: Most items we carry come with the manufacturer's warranty against defects in materials and workmanship — that covers genuine flaws like a seam that fails under normal use, a faulty zipper, or delamination, as opposed to ordinary wear or accidental damage. Warranty terms and lengths vary by brand, so the best thing you can do is keep your receipt and reach out to us at hello@trailheadsupply.co if you think something's defective; we'll help you figure out whether it's a warranty matter or a repair, and where we can, we'll handle the back-and-forth with the manufacturer on your behalf. Now, if your gear is simply worn out rather than defective — a worn-through boot sole, a zipper that's given up, a tent seam that's started to leak after many seasons — that usually isn't a warranty issue, because nothing failed; the gear just did its job for a long time. But "not under warranty" is very far from "throw it away." Almost all of that wear is fixable, and fixing it is nearly always cheaper than replacing a piece you otherwise love and that's already broken in to fit you. Bring it in and let us quote the repair. It's better for your wallet, it's better for the planet, and it's the whole heart of how we do things here. The Repairs section just below lays out what we fix and what it costs.
